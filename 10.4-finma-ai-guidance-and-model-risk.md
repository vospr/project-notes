# Course 10 · Chapter 10.4: FINMA AI guidance, Swiss data protection and model risk management

_Andrey Popov's project notes: how it works, why it was built this way, and the theory behind it._

## 2. The problem (business view, plain English)

A Swiss bank or insurer that deploys AI faces a supervisor who does not have an "AI law" to point to, but does have technology-neutral requirements on governance, risk management and internal control. FINMA says exactly this: there is no AI-specific legislation in Switzerland, and the existing principle-based requirements cover the risks of AI use (*G08* section 1).

For an AI Solutions Engineer this means building evidence, not just features: inventory entry, risk classification, test results with thresholds, documentation, monitoring and a fallback. Many Swiss banks and insurers also serve EU clients, so the EU AI Act and the EU's data rules may apply in parallel.

Finance analogy: this is the model risk framework you already know from credit and market-risk models. A pricing model has an owner, a materiality tier, an independent validation, back-testing, a limit framework and a change log. FINMA's message is that an LLM assistant is another model-like thing in the inventory, with the same questions asked in the same way, adjusted for its autonomy, opacity and dependence on third parties.

## 4. How it works (the supervisory picture)

```
        FINMA  (technology-neutral, principle-based, "same business, same risks, same rules")
          │  Risk Monitor 2023: 4 expectation areas ─► Guidance 08/2024 (Dec 2024): findings in 7 areas ─► survey (Apr 2025)
          ▼
 Institution:   Inventory ─► Materiality / risk class ─► Controls proportional to class
                   (broad AI definition,        (impact x probability)         │
                    incl. bought tools)                                        ▼
        develop / buy ─► test (accuracy, robustness, stability, bias) ─► document ─► independent review ─► approve
                                                                                            │
        run ─► monitor (performance, drift, user overrides, exceptions) ─► fallback / kill switch ─► change control ─► retire
                  └─ third parties: due diligence, contract, concentration, exit plan
```

**How to read *G08*.** It has a repeated shape: "FINMA observed ..." (a weakness seen in supervision) and "FINMA assessed whether ..." (what it looks for). It lists examples of measures, not a checklist of legal duties. Structure (*G08* contents): 1 Introduction; 2 Findings from supervision: 2.1 Governance, 2.2 Inventory and risk classification, 2.3 Data quality, 2.4 Tests and ongoing monitoring, 2.5 Documentation, 2.6 Explainability, 2.7 Independent review; 3 Outlook.

## 5. Why it is built this way (decisions and trade-offs)

Here the "decisions" are the regulator's and the institution's design choices.
| Decision | Alternatives | Why this one | Cost / risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technology-neutral, principle-based supervision (FINMA; Federal Council) | A horizontal AI law (EU model) | Same risks, same rules; avoids rules that age fast; keeps compatibility with trading partners | Less certainty for firms; interpretation by supervisory practice |
| Proportionality by materiality and probability (*G08* sec. 1) | Same controls for all AI | Effort goes where harm is largest | Needs a defensible classification method |
| Broad AI definition in the inventory (*G08* 2.2) | Only "machine learning models" | Traditional software can carry the same risks | Larger inventory, more effort |
| Independent review separate from development (*G08* 2.7) | Developers self-review | Objective challenge | Skilled reviewers are scarce |
| Pre-defined indicators and thresholds (*G08* 2.4) | Judge by impression | Prevents moving goalposts (same as my ablation) | Thresholds need calibration |
| Fallback solutions in documentation (*G08* 2.5, fn. 9) | Assume the model works | Business continuity when AI fails or drifts | Fallback must be tested and maintained |
| Extra controls and contract clauses for outsourced AI (*G08* 2.1) | Treat vendor as a black box | Responsibility stays with the institution | Negotiation power of smaller firms is limited (survey: small firms rely on external tools) |
| Sector-specific changes plus FADP as cross-sector baseline (Swiss approach) | Single AI statute | Fit with existing financial, insurance, health law | Gaps and overlaps possible |

## 6. Theory (the lecture)

### 6.1 The Swiss regulatory landscape for AI in finance
Layers, from general to specific:
1. **Constitutional and civil law, FADP** (cross-sector). The FADP in force since 1 Sept 2023 applies to AI-supported processing of personal data (FDPIC, 9 Nov 2023).
2. **Financial market law and FINMA supervision** (sector). Organisation, risk management and internal control requirements apply technology-neutrally. FINMA tools: circulars (rules of interpretation, for example 2023/1), guidance (Aufsichtsmitteilungen) reporting findings, risk monitor, supervisory dialogue, on-site reviews, audit firms' regulatory audits. Art. 89 of the Capital Adequacy Ordinance defines operational risk as the risk of loss from inappropriateness or failure of internal procedures, people or systems, or external events (cited in *G08* fn. 3); FINMA places AI risks mainly there.
3. **Professional secrecy** (for banks, the Banking Act's secrecy provision; client-identifying data handling in the operational risk circular) **(general knowledge, check)**. Important for sending prompts to external providers.
4. **International and EU rules** that reach Swiss firms (section 6.7).
5. **Federal Council policy.** On 12 February 2025 it decided to ratify the Council of Europe AI Convention, to implement it through targeted sector-specific amendments, to confine general cross-sector rules to fundamental-rights areas such as data protection, and to use non-binding instruments where possible; a consultation draft is planned by end of 2026 (law-firm summary; check current).

### 6.2 FINMA's statements on AI, in order
- **Risk Monitor 2023 (9 Nov 2023).** AI treated as a longer-term trend with four areas of challenge and expectation (FINMA AI dossier page):
  1. *Governance and responsibility*: "Clear roles and responsibilities and risk management processes must be defined and implemented"; "The responsibility for decisions cannot be delegated to AI or third parties"; sufficient AI expertise.
  2. *Robustness and reliability*: results "sufficiently accurate, robust, and reliable"; data and models and results "need to be open to critical questioning"; risks from poor data, drift from automatic optimisation, IT security with outsourcing and cloud.
  3. *Transparency and explicability*: "Institutions must ensure that the results of an application are explicable"; use of AI must be transparent to the recipient as appropriate.
  4. *No discrimination*: "Firms must avoid unjustified discrimination."
  FINMA also said that since Q4 2023 it had run on-site inspections and supervisory meetings with institutions using AI extensively, and looked at early applications in asset management, transaction monitoring and liquidity management.
- **Guidance 08/2024 (18 Dec 2024).** Findings in seven areas (section 4). Tone: observations and examples, "FINMA assessed whether".
- **Survey (24 Apr 2025).** About 400 institutions; adoption, generative AI use, third-party reliance, strategy; same business same risks same rules; contact FINMA early for critical processes or regulatory figures. Reported counts: 187 authorised institutions use AI (100 banks and securities firms, 75 insurers and intermediaries, 12 others).
- **Risk Monitor 2025 (17 Nov 2025).** Outsourcing of critical functions, cyber and ICT among nine principal risks; the press release does not mention AI. The relevance for AI is indirect: dependence on a few providers.

### 6.3 Swiss revised Data Protection Act (FADP): basics for an LLM application
Scope: personal data of natural persons (the revised act no longer covers data of legal persons; general knowledge, check). Roles: **controller** (decides purposes and means) and **processor** (processes on instruction). Key provisions I read (Fedlex text via summary; re-check wording before quoting):
- **Principles and design (Arts. 6-7).** Lawfulness, good faith, proportionality, purpose limitation, accuracy; **data protection by design and by default**: settings that limit processing to the minimum required for the purpose (Art. 7).
- **Security (Art. 8).** Technical and organisational measures appropriate to the risk.
- **Processors (Art. 9).** Delegation by contract or law only if the processor does what the controller could do itself; controller must ensure the processor can guarantee security; sub-processing needs prior approval.
- **Cross-border disclosure (Art. 16).** Allowed if the Federal Council has determined adequate protection in the destination; otherwise safeguards (treaty, notified standard clauses, specific guarantees, approved clauses or binding corporate rules).
- **Information duty and automated individual decisions (Arts. 19, 21).** The controller must inform the person of a decision based exclusively on automated processing that has legal effects or significantly affects them, and on request let them state their view and have it reviewed by a human; exceptions include contract-related decisions where the request was granted and explicit consent.
- **Profiling (Art. 5 f, g).** Profiling is any automated evaluation of personal aspects; **high-risk profiling** combines data to reveal essential aspects of a person's personality.
- **Data protection impact assessment (Art. 22).** Required beforehand when processing is likely to create a high risk to personality or fundamental rights; must describe the processing, assess the risks and set out protective measures; exceptions exist (for example legal obligation, certified systems).
- **Breach notification (Art. 24).** Notify the FDPIC as soon as possible of breaches likely to cause a high risk; processors notify controllers without delay.
- **Penalties (Art. 60 and following).** Fines up to CHF 250,000 against private persons for certain wilful breaches (for example information duties, Art. 60) — individuals, not only companies.
- Also in the act, **(general knowledge, check)**: record of processing activities (Art. 12), data protection adviser (Art. 10, optional), access right (Art. 25), privacy-policy/information duties (Art. 19).

**FDPIC position on AI (9 Nov 2023, updated 2025).** The FADP is technology-neutral and applies directly to AI-supported processing. Providers and users should make purpose, functionality and data sources transparent; people have a right to know when they talk to a machine and whether their inputs improve self-learning systems; they can object to automated processing or ask for human review of an automated decision; applications that undermine privacy, such as real-time facial recognition and social scoring, are cited as examples of unacceptable use. High-risk processing requires a DPIA.

**Implications for an advisor assistant.** Keep client-identifying data out of prompts unless approved; contract with the model provider as a processor, with no training on inputs; check where inference happens (Art. 16); DPIA if personal data is processed at scale or in a high-risk way; log with minimisation; if any decision about a person is automated, check Art. 21 duties; tell users they are talking to an AI assistant (FDPIC).

### 6.4 Model risk management (MRM) concepts
Origin: banking supervision of quantitative models. A well-known statement is the US Federal Reserve and OCC guidance SR 11-7 (2011) **(general knowledge, check)**: a model has inputs, processing and outputs; **model risk** arises from errors or misuse; controls are sound development and use, **validation** with **effective challenge** by competent independent staff, and governance with an inventory, policies and roles. FINMA's *G08* expresses the same logic for AI. Core concepts:

1. **Inventory.** A complete, central list of models and AI applications (owner, purpose, users, data, vendor, version, status, risk class). Completeness is the hard part (*G08* 2.2).
2. **Materiality / risk tiering.** Rank each application by impact and likelihood. *G08* fn. 2 lists materiality factors: significance for compliance with financial market law, financial impact, legal and reputational risk, relevance of the product, number and type of clients affected (retail vs institutional), importance for clients, consequences of errors or failure; and probability factors: complexity and explainability, type and amount of data (unstructured, personal), unsuitable development or monitoring, autonomy and process integration, dynamics (short calibration cycles), linkage of several models, potential for attacks or failures (for example from outsourcing). Illustrative scoring (my construction, not FINMA's): impact 1-3 times likelihood 1-3 gives tiers: 1-2 low, 3-4 medium, 6-9 high; controls scale with tier.
3. **Development standards and documentation.** Purpose, data, method, assumptions, limitations, performance measures, tests, controls, fallback (*G08* 2.5).
4. **Validation (before use, on change, periodically).** Conceptual soundness (is the approach appropriate), **outcomes analysis** (backtesting, out-of-sample tests, benchmark comparison), **sensitivity and stress tests**, robustness to bad inputs, bias checks (*G08* 2.4 and fn. 8). Validation is performed by people independent from development (*G08* 2.7). The usual three lines: business/development, risk and compliance, internal audit.
5. **Explainability.** Distinguish *global* (how the model behaves overall) and *local* (why this output). For classical models: feature importance, partial dependence, SHAP-type attributions, reason codes. For LLMs: internals are not explainable in this sense; use design-level explainability: retrieval evidence and citations, rule-based wrappers, structured outputs, logging of inputs and versions, reproducibility checks and human review. *G08* 2.6 asks whether results can be understood, explained and reproduced where decisions must be justified.
6. **Ongoing monitoring.** Performance against indicators, input **data drift** (population stability index PSI = Σ (a_i − e_i)·ln(a_i / e_i) over bins; rules of thumb below 0.1 stable, above 0.25 major shift; **general knowledge, check**), output quality sampling, **user override analysis**, incident and exception handling, thresholds that trigger review, retraining or fallback.
7. **Change management.** Any change of prompt, model version, retrieval index, or data feed is a change to the model; re-test proportionally; keep version history. Vendor model updates are changes you did not make: pin versions, test before adopting.
8. **Third-party and outsourcing risk.** *G08* 2.1: for outsourcing, additional tests, controls, contractual clauses on responsibility and liability, and checks that the third party has the skills. Concentration: FINMA notes dependence on providers of hardware, models and cloud in a concentrating market (*G08* sec. 2) and growing concentration at service providers of critical functions (FINMA cyber/outsourcing dossier). Practical checklist **(general knowledge, check against the applicable circular)**: due diligence, contract (audit and information rights, sub-outsourcing, data location and use, termination, liability), data protection, business continuity and tested exit/fallback plan, monitoring of the provider.
9. **Fallback and kill switch.** *G08* fn. 9: consider fallback mechanisms if the AI develops in an undesirable direction. Define who can switch off, how fast, and what the manual process is.

### 6.5 Explainability, reproducibility and "effective challenge" for LLMs
Three things an independent reviewer can actually check for an LLM assistant: (1) **Reproducibility of the process**, not bit-identical output: same inputs, versions and settings produce outputs within a measured variance; (2) **Traceability**: each answer links to the retrieved sources, prompt and model versions, guardrail results; (3) **Behavioural testing**: golden sets, adversarial and robustness tests, slices (language, product), bias checks where decisions affect people, comparison with a simpler benchmark (a rules-based or search-only system) (see 10.1 and *G08* fn. 8). **Effective challenge** means the reviewer has the competence, independence and authority to stop a deployment; evidence packages must be written for them (*G08* 2.5: documentation "recipient-oriented").

### 6.6 How the EU AI Act relates for Swiss firms serving EU clients
Facts from the Commission's page (check current; read 2026-10-10): the AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024; prohibitions and AI-literacy duties apply since 2 February 2025; obligations for general-purpose AI models since 2 August 2025; general application from 2 August 2026; the "AI Omnibus" moved the high-risk rules for Annex III use cases to 2 December 2027 and for AI embedded in regulated products (Annex I) to 2 August 2028, and entered into force on 27 July 2026 per that page. Transparency rules (Article 50) are due from August 2026 with a later date for marking synthetic content (December 2026 per the page; check current).

Risk-based structure **(general knowledge, check)**: unacceptable-risk practices are prohibited (for example social scoring by public authorities, certain biometric uses); **high-risk** systems (listed uses, including AI used to evaluate creditworthiness or credit scores of natural persons, and risk assessment and pricing for life and health insurance) must meet requirements on risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, with conformity assessment; limited-risk systems have transparency duties (tell people they interact with AI, label synthetic content); minimal-risk systems are largely unregulated; general-purpose AI models have their own obligations. Penalties are scaled by turnover **(general knowledge, check)**.

**Reach to Switzerland (general knowledge, check Art. 2).** The Act can apply to providers placing AI systems on the EU market, to deployers established in the EU, and to providers and deployers in third countries where the system's output is used in the EU. So a Swiss insurer scoring EU customers, or a Swiss vendor selling an HR or credit AI into the EU, may be in scope. Swiss firms using a general-purpose model from a US provider are usually *deployers*, with deployer duties for any high-risk use. Switzerland is **not** copying the Act (Federal Council, Feb 2025) but aims for compatibility with its main trading partners.

Also relevant for EU clients: the **GDPR** (extra-territorial reach for offering services to people in the EU) **(general knowledge, check)**.
