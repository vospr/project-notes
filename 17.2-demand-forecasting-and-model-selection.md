A forecast is useful only if it improves on an understandable alternative using information available when the decision is made. This personal demonstration predicts one week of demand for synthetic parts; it does not automate replenishment or purchasing. The work turns an earlier course example into a tested package, with explicit data checks, baseline comparisons and a promotion decision fixed before results. ## 4. How it works (technical walkthrough)

### 4.1 Start with the grain and the clock

`make_demand` creates 30 parts over 208 Monday weeks, seed 7: 6,240 raw rows. Demand combines part-specific level, trend, annual seasonality, planned overhaul effects and intermittent zeros. The generator is a reproducible teaching environment, not evidence that these assumptions represent a company's actual demand. The grain is one part-week; 6,240 is the raw count, not the number left after lag construction.

`check_demand` checks row count, nulls/parseability and event flags, duplicate keys, earliest week, latest week, nonnegative finite integer demand, part count and missing part-weeks. Row count alone is insufficient: replacing a missing key with a duplicate preserves the count. Errors expose failed check names rather than data values (`dq.py:19`).

```text
208 raw weeks x 30 parts = 6,240 rows
| 52 history weeks | 92 TRAIN | 32 VALIDATION | 32 TEST |
                    2,760       960             960
                    |-- 3 time folds --|
                    choose GBR settings
                                 compare models + fixed gate
                                                 report only after yes
```

### 4.2 Construct legal features

`add_features` sorts within part, then uses shifts of 1, 2, 4 and 52 weeks. The rolling mean is `shift(1).rolling(4).mean`: for target week t it uses t-4 through t-1. Calendar sine/cosine encode the known week of year. The overhaul flag is legal because the synthetic contract says it is planned beforehand, not because every event column is automatically safe. Dropping unavailable lag rows removes 52 weeks per part, leaving 156 weeks and 4,680 usable rows (`features.py:7`). The older reference table's label suggesting 6,240 rows "after features" must not override this arithmetic or the code.

As an illustrative calculation, earlier demands 8, 10, 6 and 12 give a legal rolling mean of 9. If current demand is 20, an unshifted last-four mean would use 10, 6, 12 and 20, giving 12. That feature partly contains the answer. The separate leak demonstration deliberately makes this mistake on validation only; its attractive score is not a competing model eligible for promotion.

### 4.3 Search inside training, then compare on validation

`search_gbr` extracts sorted unique weeks and passes those week positions to `TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=3)`. Each fold then selects all part rows belonging to the chosen weeks. Splitting the part-sorted row array directly could put different parts from the same week on opposite sides or break chronological meaning. On 92 weeks, the default three folds have 23/23, 46/23 and 69/23 train/check weeks, respectively, with expanding training windows and no extra gap.

The code manually loops over depths 3 and 6 and learning rates 0.05 and 0.1, always with 150 estimators and an explicit seed. That is four configurations times three fits, followed by one fit of the selected configuration on all 92 training weeks. It averages the three fold WAPEs equally and selects the minimum tuple `(mean_wape, depth, rate)`; an exact tie therefore has deterministic tuple ordering. This is not a call to GridSearchCV, not random cross-validation and not a search over 150 different tree counts (`train.py:56`).

The outer validation block compares last-week, seasonal-naive and last-four-mean baselines, Ridge with alpha 1.0, and the selected GBR. The best baseline and the best of Ridge/GBR are chosen on validation. Historical evidence reports GBR WAPE about 0.29831 versus best-baseline WAPE 0.32404. The separate explanation fixture's 0.36 versus 0.40 is invented test data and must not replace these historical numbers.

### 4.4 Apply the gate, then report the final block

The fixed rule is `candidate <= 0.95 * best_baseline`. Using the historical values, the required threshold is about 0.30784, and 0.29831 passes. Relative improvement is `(0.32404 - 0.29831) / 0.32404`, about 7.94%; it is neither a 7.94 percentage-point change nor measured business uplift.

`run_experiment` constructs training and validation features from pre-gate raw weeks. It materializes the final feature block only after promotion. `final_test` refuses a rejected candidate or a second call on the same result, marks the result as read and removes its stored block (`train.py:117`, `train.py:169`). Raw data is already present for input validation/fingerprinting, so "test unread" means withheld from model selection and evaluation, not physically inaccessible raw bytes. Historical final-test results are spent evidence and must not be rerun for rehearsal. The in-memory guard alone is not a durable, project-wide prevention of a new training run; process rules and the pipeline journal add a separate boundary.

## 5. Why it was built this way (decisions and trade-offs)

| Decision | Alternatives considered | Why this one | Cost / risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Synthetic data only | Employer demand | Shareable engineering demonstration | No evidence of operational suitability |
| Calendar split and week-group folds | Random rows, row-index time split | Preserve temporal availability across all parts | One synthetic regime; no interval estimate |
| Three simple baselines | Only compare complex models | Make added model complexity earn its place | Baselines do not cover every demand pattern |
| WAPE primary | MAPE, MAE, squared loss | Aggregate error remains calculable with individual zeros | High-volume items dominate; all-zero totals need care |
| Four GBR configurations | Large automated search | Bounded, inspectable reproduction of reference | Not proof the global best settings were found |
| Fixed 5% gate before results | Tune threshold after seeing scores | Separate acceptance policy from favourable reporting | No statistical significance guarantee |
| Final block after promotion | Select using test results | Protect final reporting from selection | Guard is scoped; historical reruns remain forbidden |
| Explicit evidence scopes | Say "runs everywhere" | My owner decisions and AI-assisted work remain auditable | Local Fabric tests do not establish actual integration; LLM/Teams deferred |

## 6. Theory behind it (the "lecture" part)

### 6.1 Forecast origin and available information

A forecast origin is the instant at which a prediction is made. A valid feature must be available at that instant, including its real reporting delay. A date is known in advance; a realised sale is known only later. An apparently historical value revised after the origin can still leak information. Here lagged demand and the declared planned flag model availability, but no real late-arrival or revision process is demonstrated.

The backtest is repeated one-week-ahead prediction: later validation weeks may use actual demand from earlier validation weeks. That is legitimate if those observations have arrived by each new origin. It is not evidence for predicting all 32 weeks at the start of the block. A fixed 32-week forecast would need recursive predictions, direct horizon-specific models or another explicitly tested design. This distinction is central to the project's feature construction.

### 6.2 WAPE, zeros and weighting

The implementation is `sum(abs(y-p)) / max(sum(abs(y)), 1e-9)`. For illustrative actuals [0, 10, 20] and predictions [2, 8, 18], absolute error totals 6 and actual volume totals 30, so WAPE is 0.20. Individual zero demand does not create a division by zero as it would in ordinary per-row MAPE. A zero prediction everywhere gives WAPE 1 when total absolute actual demand is positive.

For an all-zero actual block, zero predictions yield 0; nonzero predictions yield their absolute total divided by 1e-9, potentially enormous. The epsilon supplies numerical behavior, not a meaningful percentage interpretation. The helper does not itself validate finite inputs; the surrounding checks matter. Because the denominator is aggregate volume, high-volume parts dominate, and WAPE can exceed 1. The project uses MAE in demand units and signed bias `mean(prediction-actual)` as diagnostics, not as gate substitutes.

### 6.3 Fitting loss differs from selection metric

Boosting builds an additive predictor, conceptually `F_m = F_(m-1) + learning_rate * tree_m`, where successive trees correct remaining fitting errors. Depth controls interaction complexity and learning rate controls each stage's contribution. The project leaves GBR's fitting loss at its squared-error default while selecting configurations by WAPE; "optimises WAPE" is therefore accurate only for selection, not the training objective. The [official estimator reference](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.ensemble.GradientBoostingRegressor.html) documents those parameters. Here the manual loop makes the distinction visible.

### 6.4 Nested time decisions and uncertainty

Inner folds choose GBR settings; outer validation chooses the family and gate result; the final block reports the chosen result. This hierarchy reduces test contamination but does not make validation an untouched performance estimate. Repeatedly trying new ideas after observing validation can still overfit it. The [TimeSeriesSplit reference](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.TimeSeriesSplit.html) explains expanding temporal splits; this project applies them to weeks, then expands weeks to rows.

An equally weighted mean of fold WAPEs differs from pooling all fold errors and denominators. If illustrative folds have errors/volumes 10/100 and 20/50, the mean ratios are 0.25, while the pooled ratio is 30/150 = 0.20. Neither automatically measures deployment utility; choose deliberately. Here equal-fold averaging is actual code behavior. Correlation within weeks, one synthetic generator and no confidence intervals limit claims about generalisation.

### 6.5 A policy gate is not a hypothesis test

A relative margin expresses the minimum acceptable practical change against a baseline. Equality passes because the operator is <=. Statistical significance instead depends on sampling uncertainty and a specified inferential procedure. Passing this gate neither proves stable future improvement nor authorises business action. This owner-defined demonstration gate is different from the later monitoring rule, which uses strict greater-than comparisons.

### 6.6 Ridge and the linear baseline

Ridge fits a linear predictor while penalising large coefficients: minimise `sum((y - Xw)^2) + alpha * sum(w^2)`. The penalty reduces sensitivity to correlated predictors at the cost of bias. It is not a feature-selection rule that normally forces coefficients exactly to zero; Lasso has a different penalty. Ridge can combine lag effects economically, while trees can express interactions and nonlinear thresholds. See the [official Ridge reference](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.linear_model.Ridge.html). In this case alpha is fixed at 1.0, no scaling pipeline is used, and only GBR settings are searched. Therefore the comparison does not prove every linear model loses to boosting: scaling and a deliberately bounded alpha search are possible future experiments, not completed work.

### 6.7 Ablation and robustness answer different questions

Permutation importance perturbs a fitted model's inputs to measure reliance; correlated lag features can substitute for one another. Retrained ablation removes a feature group and refits the model, asking whether a simpler model retains useful performance. Robustness asks whether a conclusion persists across declared regimes, seeds, forecast origins and important segments. Neither exercise is supplied by one successful fit. In this case, a proposed ablation could compare lag-only, lag-plus-seasonality and lag-plus-planned-event variants under the same chronological validation design. A robustness study could vary synthetic intermittency and seasonality using a pre-agreed generator family. These are proposals: do not run them against the spent historical final block or claim the existing study already did so. A new study needs a separately agreed untouched evaluation design and no tuning of the fixed historical gate.

## 8. Glossary

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| Grain | One synthetic part and Monday week per raw row |
| Forecast origin | Time at which information is frozen for a prediction |
| Lag | A target value from an earlier week within the same part |
| Leakage | Information used that was unavailable at the forecast origin or improperly used for selection |
| Expanding fold | Later check block preceded by a growing training window |
| Hyperparameter | A setting chosen outside ordinary parameter fitting |
