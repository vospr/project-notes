# Course 3 · Chapter 3.1: Why "felt faster" isn't evidence: METR, DORA, counter-metrics

_Andrey Popov's project notes: how it works, why it was built this way, and the theory behind it._

**In short:** Why 'AI made us faster' needs evidence: METR found developers believed they were 20% faster but were 19% slower, and DORA frames AI as an amplifier. Argues for a tiered metric set pairing every speed metric with a quality counter-metric.

## 2. The problem (business view, plain English)

An internal AI-first programme on a client account wanted to show that AI coding agents make delivery faster. Its dashboard counted AI adoption through ticket labels written by agents. Those labels were empty for most of the history and say only *whether* AI touched a ticket. Throughput came from story points. There was no cost, no record of who performed each step, and nothing finer than monthly time, while the programme's stated goal was feedback loops of minutes to hours.

Without better measures, "AI made us faster" cannot be told apart from "AI made us feel faster". The best controlled study available found developers believed they were about 20% faster and were measured about 19% slower.

Finance analogy: a fund manager says "my new process feels more profitable". You do not accept that. You ask for a backtest on audited returns, and you ask for the risk next to the return (drawdown, volatility). Speed without quality is return without risk.

## 4. How it works (the argument, step by step)

This chapter is about the *design logic* of the metric set, not code.

```
 "AI made us faster"           claim by feel / survey / labels
        │
        ▼  METR: perception ≠ measurement          → need platform-observed signals
        ▼  DORA: AI amplifies the system            → need system-level, paired metrics
        ▼  GitClear/DORA: instability, duplication  → need counter-metrics
        ▼  Dashboard audit: grain monthly, no cost, no step owner, MR feed dark
        │
        ▼  Tiered metric set
   Tier 0 today ─ Tier 1 config ─ Tier 2 experiments ─ Tier 3 cost
        every speed metric ⇄ one quality counter-metric
```

**Step 1: why self-report fails.** METR: developers forecast 24% faster, reported 20% faster after, measured 19% slower. Perception was off by about 39 points. So sentiment or survey results go in the report as sentiment, "never as effect".

**Step 2: what the industry measures.** Roughly eight metrics, five of which need merge-request data. In this account that feed had been switched off since May 2026, so the group could not adopt standard practice at all. This reframed the ask from "a metric is broken" to "this blocks standard practice".

**Step 3: pair every speed metric with a counter-metric.** The practitioner table I adopted:

| Speed metric | Quality counter-metric |
|---|---|
| Acceptance rate | % of AI code rewritten in review |
| Code generation speed | Post-merge defect rate |
| Test generation volume | Mutation score (not coverage) |
| Deployment frequency | Change failure rate |
| PR cycle time | Number of review rounds |

**Step 4: what not to measure.** Acceptance rate as a headline, lines of code, per-developer comparison, any story-point-derived rate presented as before/after evidence. The per-developer exclusion is stated to be my own objection, not a published finding.

**Step 5: the tiered set.** Tier 0 needs no new instrumentation (lead/cycle ratio, duplication, defect leakage and reopen rate, created vs resolved). Tier 1 needs configuration. Tier 2 (execution attribution, gate pass rate, bypass rate) is labelled an experiment. Tier 3 is cost per successful merge and cache-read ratio.

**Step 6: what the lead changed.** The lead rejected my "self-reported labels" framing, rejected including Claude and Codex in the *historical* baseline (no history exists), set the baseline at the top five metrics, and redirected the trial to the platform streams. I kept cost as a forward-looking track.

## 5. Why it was built this way (decisions and trade-offs)

| Decision | Alternatives | Why this one | Cost / risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Argue from METR plus DORA, not from vendor ROI claims | Vendor ROI studies | One randomised, measured result beats surveys; DORA gives the system view | Single small study; DORA is survey-based |
| Counter-metric pairing | Speed metrics alone | Speed metrics are gameable (small deploys inflate frequency) and can hide instability | More metrics to maintain; the pair can be split by data windows ( leakage reaches January 2026, its reopen-rate counterweight does not) |
| Label proxies as proxies | Present proxies as the real thing | A review vote is not reviewer pickup | Less impressive headlines |
| Execution attribution as the new adoption metric | Keep the label boolean | Turns adoption into a ratio over a known denominator, observable from PRs and pipelines | No prior art; "own the burden of proving it". Not built |
| Exclude per-developer comparisons | Report per person | Gaming and trust risk; the AI-spend half is individually sensitive | Cannot answer "who benefits most" |
| Baseline from what the dashboard really holds | Promise 2025 vs 2026 vs now | Windows are server-side limits; only 0 of 5 metrics reach January 2025 | The AC wording could not be met as written |

## 6. Theory behind it (the "lecture" part)

### 6.1 Perception, measurement and the 'felt faster' trap
A claim about productivity has three possible sources: forecast (before), self-report (after), and measurement (instrumented outcome). They differ by **measurement error** and by **bias**. Self-report is subject to recall bias, effort illusion (work that feels engaging feels fast) and demand effects. A **randomised controlled trial** (RCT) assigns the treatment at random, so confounders balance on average, and measures the outcome with a clock. METR randomised *tasks* to "AI allowed" or "AI not allowed" for the same developers, and timed them.

Limits of any such study: small n (16 developers), non-representative population (experts on their own large repositories), tool and model vintage, and learning effects. A result of "−19%" is a point estimate with a wide interval, not a law.

*In my project:* the METR gap (about 39 points between report and measurement) is why sentiment is labelled sentiment, and why I want platform-observed signals.

### 6.2 The DORA metrics and what they do and do not see
DORA's four "key" delivery measures are: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, and time to restore service. The first two are throughput; the last two are stability. The 2025 report found AI's association with throughput turned positive but its association with instability stayed negative, and framed AI as an **amplifier**: it magnifies what is already strong or weak in the delivery system. It moved from low/medium/high/elite tiers to seven team archetypes.

Two structural weaknesses when you apply DORA to AI: (1) AI can raise deployment frequency simply by producing more code, so frequency rises while failure rate degrades; (2) visible targets invite small deployments that inflate the count. DORA is also mostly survey-based.

### 6.3 Goodhart's law and counter-metrics
**Goodhart's law:** when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The practical response is **paired indicators**: put a metric that is cheap to inflate next to one that gets worse when the first is gamed. Properties of a good counter-metric: it measures a different failure mode, it is measured independently of the speed metric, and it has a clean history.

Examples: PR cycle time ⇄ review rounds; deployment frequency ⇄ change failure rate; test volume ⇄ mutation score (coverage can be raised by tests that assert nothing).

Finance analogy: return paired with drawdown. A strategy that doubles return by doubling tail risk has not improved.

*In my project:* the comparison rule is only satisfiable where a clean pre-AI series exists: duplication, defect leakage and reopen rate have long clean histories, labels do not.

### 6.4 Unit of analysis and denominators
A metric is a ratio; the denominator decides what it means. "Per developer", "per line", "per story point", "per ticket", "per merged change" answer different questions and have different failure modes. Story points are an estimate, not a unit of output: if estimates inflate, cost per point falls with no real change. Lines of code reward verbosity. Per-developer rankings invite gaming and are sensitive data.

*In my project:* the exclusions in the metric set follow this; chapter 3.2 covers the unit I chose.

### 6.5 Baselines, comparison design and what "evidence" requires
To claim an effect you need a counterfactual. The strongest design is randomisation; next is a controlled before/after comparison with the same team, same task type, same review path and same time window. Without it, numbers *describe* but do not *prove*. A baseline must also be reachable: a trailing server-side window means older history disappears.

*In my project:* the comparison rule is unsatisfiable on the label track (labels began April 2026) and satisfiable on duplication, leakage and reopen rate. The review's "if built today" says to design the comparison first.

### 6.6 Evidence grading
Label every claim by strength: **empirical** (measured, stated sample), **framework** (a model), **vendor** (conflict of interest), **local** (our own data), **judgement**. Do not mix grades in one sentence.

*In my project:* the research note tags every claim this way. Missing tags would have made the 'DORA pattern exactly' sentence harder to challenge.

## 8. Glossary

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| METR study | Randomised trial (16 experienced developers, 246 tasks) finding a 19% slowdown despite a 20% perceived speed-up |
| DORA | Research programme defining delivery performance by throughput and stability measures |
| Lead time vs cycle time | Lead = request to delivery; cycle = work start to delivery; the gap is waiting |
| Counter-metric | A quality or stability measure paired with a speed measure to expose gaming |
| Goodhart's law | A measure used as a target stops being a good measure |
| Perception gap | Difference between what people believe happened and what was measured |
| Execution attribution | Recording whether an agent, a human or automation performed each lifecycle step |
| Label coverage | Share of tickets that carry an AI label at all |
| Sentiment vs effect | Survey results measure how people feel, not what happened |
| Amplifier framing | DORA 2025: AI magnifies existing strengths and weaknesses of a delivery system |
| Cost per successful merge | Spend divided by changes that actually merged; a ratio, not a distribution |
