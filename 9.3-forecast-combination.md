**In short:** Forecast combination with equal, R2-weighted and constrained least squares weights. In-sample CLS scored MSE 55.4 against 57.4 for equal weights, but on 2024 the simple average beat every single model, illustrating the combination puzzle.

## 2. The problem (business view, plain English)

Each model makes errors. If two models make *different* errors, averaging them cancels part of the error. The thesis asks whether blending classical and neural forecasts beats any single one in the Swiss market. The business use is robustness: the best model in one year may fail the next, an average is a hedge against picking the wrong one.

Finance analogy: portfolio diversification. Two assets with imperfectly correlated returns give a portfolio with lower variance than either. Forecasts are assets, forecast errors are returns. The same trap applies too: optimal portfolio weights estimated from noisy covariances often do worse out of sample than 1/N.

## 4. How it works (technical walkthrough)

```
5–6 frozen models ──► prediction matrix P (T × N, price levels)
                         │
     equal:  w_i = 1/N           (NB c25)
     R²:     w_i = R²_i / ΣR²    (NB c26)
     CLS:    min_w Σ_t (y_t − P_t·w)²  s.t. Σw=1, 0≤w≤1   SLSQP, start 1/N   (NB c21)
                         │
              combined forecast  P·w  →  MSE / MAPE / R²  on the same y
```

**CLS function.** Objective is the sum of squared errors; one equality constraint (weights sum to 1); bounds (0, 1); SLSQP from equal weights. If the optimiser does not report success it raises.

## 5. Why it was built this way (decisions and trade-offs)

| Decision | Alternatives | Why this one | Cost / risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Combine instead of picking one model | Pick the best on validation | Literature: combinations are robust | Needs diverse, non-redundant members |
| CLS with weights ≥ 0, sum 1 | Unconstrained regression; LAD; inverse-MSE | Interpretable weights, follows Weron and co-authors | Fitted on the test period here; SLSQP fragile on an unscaled objective |
| Equal weights as a scheme | Trimmed mean; median | Simplest benchmark, "surprisingly robust" | Included the worst model (ARX) |
| R²-proportional weights | Inverse-MSE | Easy to compute | R²s are close (0.62–0.74), so weights ≈ equal; negative R² breaks it (2024) |
| Fit weights on the 30% block | Fit on a held-out validation block before | Only one split was made | In-sample; not usable in real time |
| Re-fit CLS on 2024 | Apply the 2020 weights | Quick | Again in-sample; stalled at the start point |
| Drop the plain NN in 2024 | Keep all members | It failed badly | Selection on test data |

## 6. Theory behind it (the "lecture" part)

### 6.1 Why combining works (Bates-Granger and the diversity identity)
Take two unbiased forecasts with error variances σ₁², σ₂² and error correlation ρ. The combination w·f₁ + (1−w)·f₂ has error variance w²σ₁² + (1−w)²σ₂² + 2w(1−w)ρσ₁σ₂. Minimising over w gives
w* = (σ₂² − ρσ₁σ₂) / (σ₁² + σ₂² − 2ρσ₁σ₂).
For N forecasts, w* = Σ⁻¹ι / (ι'Σ⁻¹ι), where Σ is the error covariance matrix and ι a vector of ones (Bates & Granger 1969).
*Example:* σ₁ = 1, σ₂ = 1.5, ρ = 0.3. Then w₁* = 0.77 and the variance is 0.87. Equal weights give 1.04, worse than model 1 alone (1.00). If σ₁ = σ₂, equal weights are optimal for any ρ.

For squared error and equal weights there is an exact identity: (y − f̄)² = (1/N)Σ(y − f_i)² − (1/N)Σ(f_i − f̄)². The average's error equals the average of the members' errors minus their **diversity** (spread around the mean). It guarantees the average beats the *average* member, never the best one.

*In my project:* on 2024 the five members' MSEs average 918.4 and the equal-weight blend scores 335.4, so diversity is 583.

### 6.2 Weighting schemes
- **Equal weights** 1/N: no parameters to estimate.
- **Performance-based** (inverse-MSE, R²-proportional): weights ∝ 1/MSE_i; R² is a poor signal when all R² are similar, and meaningless when negative.
- **CLS / regression weights:** min Σ(y − Pw)² with w ≥ 0, Σw = 1; Granger-Ramanathan (1984) discuss unconstrained, no-intercept and constrained versions. Constraints reduce variance; w ≥ 0 gives sparse solutions when forecasts are collinear.
- **Robust variants:** LAD, median, trimmed mean (compared by Weron and Misiorek, *TH p.12*).

*In my project:* CLS with SLSQP; equal and R² schemes as in section 4.

### 6.3 Estimation error and the "forecast combination puzzle"
Empirically, simple averages often beat sophisticated weighting schemes out of sample (Stock & Watson 2004; Timmermann 2006). The puzzle: if optimal weights differ from 1/N, why does 1/N win? Standard explanations: (1) **estimation error:** estimating N weights from T observations adds about σ²·N/T to the out-of-sample MSE, and weights that are close to equal gain little from being estimated (Smith & Wallis 2009); (2) forecast errors have similar variances and high correlations, so the optimum is near equal weights anyway; (3) **instability:** the optimal weights change over time, so weights fitted in the past are stale (Claeskens et al. 2016 formalise the random-weights version). Atiya (2020), cited in the thesis, adds that averaging reduces variance from model uncertainty.

Careful: the puzzle concerns **out-of-sample** performance of estimated weights. A comparison where the weights are fitted on the scored data removes the estimation-error cost and says nothing about the puzzle.

*In my project:* the in-sample CLS (55.4) beats equal (57.4) by 3.7%, as it must; on 2024 the re-fit CLS collapsed to equal weights. So "the simple average won" is true only in the sense that on 2024 it came out ahead of every single model; CLS was never tested against it fairly.

### 6.4 Out-of-sample discipline for combinations
Weights must be estimated from **past forecast errors** and applied forward. Standard schemes: expanding or rolling windows of past forecasts (Nowotarski, Raviv, Trück & Weron 2014 compare such schemes for electricity prices), a short calibration window (e.g. the last 28 days), or fixed equal weights. Member models must also be trained on data before the weight-fitting window.

*In my project:* members trained on the first 70%; weights fitted on the last 30%, which is also where they are scored. A valid split would hold out a third block: train members, fit weights, score.

### 6.5 Metrics and the Diebold-Mariano test
- **MSE/RMSE** punish large errors, appropriate when spikes cost money; **MAE** is robust to spikes; **MAPE** divides by the actual, so near-zero or negative prices explode it (sMAPE and rMAE, relative to a naive forecast, are safer). R² on the same test set is a decreasing function of MSE, not a separate metric.
- **Diebold-Mariano (1995):** for forecast errors e₁,t and e₂,t and loss L (e.g. squared error), let d_t = L(e₁,t) − L(e₂,t). DM = d̄ / √(LRV/T), where the long-run variance LRV is estimated HAC-style because d_t is autocorrelated; the statistic is compared with N(0,1) (Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold 1997 give a small-sample correction). H0: equal accuracy. In day-ahead work it is usually applied to daily loss differences or per hour, so that T counts days.
- Illustration with made-up numbers: 328 days, mean daily loss gap 8, standard deviation of the gap 40 → DM = 8/(40/√328) ≈ 3.6. The same mean gap with standard deviation 150 gives 0.96, not significant.

*In my project:* no DM test, so no statement of significance exists for any of the gaps in 9.3 (57.3 vs 57.4; 55.4 vs 57.4; 297 vs 305). Use the epftoolbox implementation next time.

## 8. Glossary

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| Forecast combination | Weighted average of several models' forecasts |
| Bates-Granger weights | Weights minimising the combined error variance, w* = Σ⁻¹ι/(ι'Σ⁻¹ι) |
| CLS | Least-squares weights constrained to be non-negative and sum to 1 |
| SLSQP | Sequential least-squares programming, SciPy's constrained optimiser |
| Diversity | Spread of member forecasts around their average |
| Combination puzzle | Simple averages often beat estimated "optimal" weights out of sample |
| In-sample weights | Weights fitted on the same data used to score the blend |
| Diebold-Mariano test | Tests equal predictive accuracy of two forecast series |
| LRV / HAC | Long-run variance estimator robust to autocorrelation |
| MAE / RMSE | Average absolute / root-mean-squared error |
| MAPE / sMAPE | Percentage errors; MAPE blows up near zero actuals |
| rMAE | MAE relative to a naive benchmark |
| Selection on the test set | Choosing models or members after seeing test scores |
