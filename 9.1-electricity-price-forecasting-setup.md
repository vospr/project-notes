_Andrey Popov's project notes: how it works, why it was built this way, and the theory behind it._

**In short:** The thesis setup: forecasting Swiss hourly day-ahead prices from 26,304 rows, with cleaning, lag features, an ADF stationarity test (-8.39), a chronological 70/30 split and HAC errors. Defines the day-ahead information set that same-day features violate.

## 2. The problem (business view, plain English)

In Switzerland (and most of Europe) the price of electricity for each hour of tomorrow is fixed today in one auction. All 24 prices of day D are set together around midday on D−1 (standard market design; the exact Swiss gate-closure time is not in the sources, and the thesis never mentions the auction). Traders, hedgers and plant operators need a good guess of tomorrow's 24 prices before the auction closes. A better guess is worth money.

The thesis asks whether blending classical econometric forecasts with a neural network beats any single one, in a market that is less studied than the Nordic or British ones. This chapter is the foundation: the data, the tests, and the rule for what the model may know.

Finance analogy: back-testing a trading strategy for an opening auction. The one rule that matters is that every input must be something you would have had at order-submission time. A number that was only published after the close is look-ahead bias, and it makes any back-test look better than it was.

## 5. Why it was built this way (decisions and trade-offs)

| Decision | Alternatives | Why this one | Cost / risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| One model for all 24 hours | 24 hourly models (Weron) | Simplicity; follows Kristiansen | Hourly models are usually more accurate |
| No differencing | Difference the log price | ADF rejects a unit root | ADF run on cleaned 2018–2020 only |
| Log transform + clamp to 0.01 | asinh transform; keep negatives | Easy; keeps prices positive | Clamping distorts the very low prices; MAPE explodes (9.4) |
| Spikes replaced by median (|z|>2.5) | Keep spikes; robust loss | Literature precedent | Hardest hours removed from the test targets too |
| Lags 24/48/168 | Only 24 | Standard EPF choice | None; all legal |
| Daily max as day-level signal | Daily min or mean | "More significant" in own comparison | Comparison was run on the same-day value, so it partly measured the leak |
| HAC(24) standard errors | Plain OLS errors | Residuals are autocorrelated (DW 0.547) | Only fixes inference, not the point forecast |
| Single 70/30 chronological split | Rolling daily re-fit | Simple; no shuffling | No recalibration; the "adaptive testing scheme" in *TH p.20* is not in the code |
| 2024 data as robustness test | None | Out-of-period check | Different outlier rule, different regime |

## 6. Theory behind it (the "lecture" part)

### 6.1 The day-ahead auction and the information set
In a day-ahead market all 24 hourly prices of day D come out of one auction on D−1. A forecaster stands at a fixed time (before gate closure on D−1) and must predict 24 numbers at once. The **information set** I_t is everything published by then: all prices up to day D−1, past generation, and forecasts of load, wind and solar for day D (forecasts, not actuals). A model is a valid day-ahead forecaster only if every input is in I_t. Consequences: price lags of 24 h or more are fine for all 24 target hours; "yesterday's average" is fine; "today's maximum" and "this hour's actual production" are not. Experiments that respect this are called **pseudo out-of-sample**. Hourly prices are strongly correlated within a day, so many authors model the day as a 24-dimensional vector rather than 24 unrelated series.

*In my project:* the thesis states the right design (previous-day max, lagged infeed, *TH p.22*) but the code uses same-day values.

### 6.2 Stationarity and the ADF test
A series is **weakly stationary** if its mean and variance are constant and its autocovariance depends only on the lag. A **unit root** series (random walk, y_t = y_{t−1} + e_t) is not: shocks never die out, and regressions on it can show "spurious" relations.

The augmented Dickey-Fuller test fits
Δy_t = α + γ·y_{t−1} + Σ_{i=1..p} δ_i·Δy_{t−i} + e_t.
H0: γ = 0 (unit root). H1: γ < 0 (stationary). The t-statistic on γ does not follow a normal or Student distribution; it follows the Dickey-Fuller distribution, tabulated by MacKinnon. For a constant-only regression and large n the critical values are about −3.43 (1%), −2.86 (5%), −2.57 (10%), which are exactly the numbers in the output. More negative is stronger evidence. Lags p absorb autocorrelation in Δy; choose by AIC/BIC up to a maximum.

Limits you must know: (a) low power against near-unit-root series, so "reject" is strong but "fail to reject" is weak; (b) deterministic seasonality and structural breaks can mislead it, so pair it with KPSS (H0 is stationarity) and look at the plot; (c) the test says nothing about the future regime. Example: a log price with AR coefficient 0.98 is stationary but nearly impossible to separate from 1 in short samples.

*In my project:* t = −8.39 against −3.43 is a clear rejection on 2018–2020; "no differencing" follows. The test is on one regime and one cleaned sample.

### 6.3 Autocorrelation, ACF and Ljung-Box
The sample autocorrelation at lag k is ρ̂_k. For white noise of length n, ρ̂_k is approximately N(0, 1/n), so the 95% band is ±1.96/√n. The **Ljung-Box** statistic tests the first m lags jointly:
Q = n(n+2) Σ_{k=1..m} ρ̂_k² / (n−k), compared with χ² with m − (number of fitted ARMA parameters) degrees of freedom. H0: no autocorrelation up to lag m. Remarks: with n in the tens of thousands, even tiny autocorrelations reject H0, so read the size of ρ̂_k as well as the p-value; apply it to residuals, not raw prices; correct the degrees of freedom for fitted parameters.

*In my project:* with n = 18,294 the band is ±0.0145, not the ±0.081 drawn in the notebook; the test result itself was never displayed.

### 6.4 HAC (Newey-West) standard errors
OLS estimates β̂ = (X'X)⁻¹X'y. If errors are autocorrelated or heteroskedastic, the usual variance formula is wrong, so t-tests and p-values mislead. The HAC estimator keeps the sandwich form
V = (X'X)⁻¹ S (X'X)⁻¹, S = Γ₀ + Σ_{l=1..L} w_l (Γ_l + Γ_l'), w_l = 1 − l/(L+1) (Bartlett weights),
where Γ_l is the sample autocovariance of the score x_t·û_t. L is the truncation lag; a rule of thumb is about n^¼ or a multiple of the seasonal period. HAC changes **standard errors only**. The coefficients and the point forecasts are exactly the OLS ones. It does not cure bias, omitted variables, or a rank-deficient design.

*In my project:* HAC(24) gives the p-values in Table 5. It has no effect on any forecast number in the thesis, and the design is rank-deficient (9.4), so most p-values there are not identified.

### 6.5 Log prices, negative prices, back-transformation
Logs stabilise variance and make effects multiplicative. They require positive prices, but day-ahead prices can be zero or negative. Common fixes: clamp (used here), shift by a constant, or the **asinh** transform, which is roughly linear near 0 and logarithmic in the tails (Uniejewski, Weron & Ziel, 2018, already in the thesis reference list, *TH p.44*). A model fitted on ln y and exponentiated gives exp(E[ln y]), which is a median-type forecast; the mean needs a smearing factor (Duan 1983), about exp(σ²/2) for normal errors.

*In my project:* residual variance of the ARX in logs is 0.026, so the factor would be about 1.013, small; the thesis notes that the combined forecast "underestimates prices". Whether this was tested is not in the sources.

### 6.6 Evaluating a forecast honestly
Three habits: (1) split **chronologically**, never shuffle; (2) compare with **naive benchmarks** (same hour yesterday, same hour last week), because R² of 0.7 means little if "yesterday" gets 0.55; (3) prefer **rolling re-estimation** (fit on the past, forecast the next day, move on), which matches how a forecaster would actually work and tests many days instead of one stretch.

*In my project:* one 70/30 split, no naive benchmark in the thesis; the review's re-run shows naive rules at MSE 65–83 against about 45 for the corrected regression.

## 8. Glossary

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| Day-ahead market | Auction on D−1 that sets the 24 hourly prices of day D |
| Information set | Everything known at forecast time |
| Look-ahead bias / leakage | Using inputs that were not available at forecast time |
| Pseudo out-of-sample | Evaluation that uses only data available at each forecast time |
| Stationarity (weak) | Constant mean and variance; autocovariance depends only on lag |
| Unit root | y_t = y_{t−1} + e_t: shocks are permanent |
| ADF test | Tests H0 "unit root" with a regression including lagged differences |
| KPSS | Stationarity test with the opposite null |
| ACF | Correlation of a series with its own lags |
| Ljung-Box Q | Joint test that the first m autocorrelations are zero |
| HAC / Newey-West | Standard errors robust to heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation |
| Durbin-Watson | Lag-1 residual autocorrelation statistic; about 2 means none |
| Clamp | Replace values below a floor with the floor |
| asinh transform | Log-like transform that also accepts zero and negative values |
| Naive benchmark | Forecast = same hour yesterday or last week |
| EPF | Electricity price forecasting |
