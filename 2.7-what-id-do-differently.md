## 2. The problem (business view, plain English)

The project is a good story only if the lessons are real. This chapter is the answer sheet for "what went wrong and what now".

Finance analogy: SOX distinguishes testing the **design** of a control (is it written down and sensible?) from testing its **operating effectiveness** (does it actually run, every time, with evidence?). Verdaca had many controls well designed on paper (14 stages, 12 gates, signed-off summaries) whose operation nobody observed until the redo ran one deliberation end to end. The lesson is: look at operation, not at the memo.

## 5. Why it was built this way (decision table)

| Decision | Alternatives | Why it happened | What I'd change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Layered build: ports, kernel, auth, gateway, channels, then engine | Thin end-to-end slice first | Agents optimised "is the stage done?"; I asked that question | Ask "what does a real request do?" at every stage; record one run per stage |
| Seven seams up front | Two (memory, channel) | Seams were the thesis; swaps are the best demo | Add a seam on the second real use |

| Stage-level summaries as the control | Observed runs | Cheap to read | Done = a run with a receipt |
| Self-scored benchmark | Blind, cross-model | One session, easy to produce | Different judge model plus human subset |
| Docs and README written by agents | Human-owned claims | Fast | A claim-to-evidence table in the README (done in the redo) |
| Redo as one cheap slice | Full roadmap | The review priced step 1 at 1-2 days | Keep to one slice, then stop |
| Stop after step 1 | Continue to benchmark | No consumer, token and time cost | State the stopping rule in advance |

## 6. Theory behind it (the "lecture" part)

### 6.1 Tracer bullets and the walking skeleton
A **walking skeleton** (Cockburn) is the thinnest implementation that performs a real end-to-end function and can be built, deployed and tested; a **tracer bullet** (Hunt and Thomas, *The Pragmatic Programmer*) is the same idea: fire a real round through all layers early to see where it lands. The alternative, building layer by layer, defers integration risk to the end and produces components that each look finished. The point is *feedback on the riskiest assumption first*.

*In my project:* the riskiest assumption was "producer, blind reviewer and synthesizer give something worth recording". It was tested last, in about 1,000 lines.

### 6.2 YAGNI, the rule of three and the cost of a wrong abstraction
YAGNI ("you aren't gonna need it"): do not build for a need you do not have. The **rule of three**: abstract after the third similar case, not the first. Sandi Metz's formulation: duplication is cheaper than the wrong abstraction, because wrong abstractions attract callers and are expensive to unwind. A port is an abstraction with a price: mapping code, a test suite, a style to keep consistent.

*In my project:* seven ports, two with a second adapter; the LiteLLM port is "a second abstraction layer".

### 6.3 Goodhart's law and agents that optimise the evaluator
Goodhart's law (in Marilyn Strathern's wording): when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. An AI coding agent optimises what you ask it to report; if the report is "stage complete", it will produce stage-complete evidence. Counter-measures: measure the outcome, make the check independent of the producer, sample reality (run the thing), and keep the checker's incentives different from the producer's.

*In my project:* stage summaries said "complete" while the live path made one model call. The article attributes the general finding to a 2026 paper; I have not checked it.

### 6.4 Design effectiveness versus operating effectiveness
In internal-control testing, **design effectiveness** asks whether a control, if performed as described, would prevent or detect the risk; **operating effectiveness** asks whether it was performed, consistently, with evidence, over the period. A control can be well designed and never operate. Evidence for operation is observed runs, logs and re-performance, not the procedure manual.

*In my project:* gates and gateway were designed in detail; the first operating evidence of the deliberation is the 2026-10-01 receipts.

### 6.5 Work in progress, batch size and documentation as inventory
Lean software thinking (Poppendieck; Reinertsen): unfinished work is inventory with carrying costs, large batches hide defects, and feedback delay multiplies rework. Documentation is also inventory: each page must be kept consistent with code, or it becomes a source of false claims (documentation drift). Planning prose can be cheap to produce with agents and expensive to keep true.

*In my project:* README badges and site copy diverged from the code; the redo's fix was a claim-to-evidence table (README "Real deliberation").

### 6.6 Valid experiments: confounds, sample size and confidence
A comparison is credible when (a) the arms differ only in the treatment, (b) the grader is independent of the producers and blind to the arm, (c) the sample is large enough for the effect, (d) compute is matched. For a mean difference, the 95% confidence interval is about `mean ± t * s / sqrt(n)`; with n = 10, t is about 2.26, so the interval is wide unless the effect is large and the variance small. A relative improvement ("+47%") means nothing without its baseline value and its interval. Position, verbosity and self-preference bias in LLM judges push results toward elaborate or own-style outputs.

*In my project:* "+47%" came from one session playing every role and scoring itself, N=10; the three redo runs are N=3 on one question and make no quality claim.

### 6.7 Calibration loss in summarisation
When a model compresses a text, qualifiers ("may", "in the excerpt", "likely") are the first thing to go, because they add words and subtract from a clean message. The failure is **uncertainty laundering**: hedged inputs, confident output. Defences: require the summary to carry each caveat or flag its removal; check by an independent reader; evaluate with a fixture of hedged sources and a rubric ("does every hedge survive?"). The article cites a 2025 study on LLM summaries of papers generalising beyond their sources; I have not checked it.

*In my project:* the Desktop receipt's final answer states "the evidence shows ... no confidence intervals" where the reviewer wrote "shown" (excerpt-bound) (2.

|---|---|
